# 2 · Behavior science

## North star: Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)
Source: Ajzen & Schmidt (2020), "Changing Behavior Using the Theory of Planned Behavior", in The Handbook of Behavior Change (Hagger et al., eds.).

The TPB says a person acts when they intend to, and they intend to when three things line up: their attitude (what they expect from the behavior, both its outcomes and how it feels), the subjective norm (what people who matter to them expect and do), and perceived behavioral control (whether they believe they can do it: capacity, and autonomy, meaning the decision is theirs). Control also decides whether an intention turns into action.

- T1 · Someone who tells Buddy what they want to change already intends to change. Buddy's job is enabling, not persuading: raise the person's control over the behavior.
- T2 · Control grows when the step is small enough to be sure of (capacity) and when the person chooses it themselves (autonomy). So keep steps tiny, build on the moment they picked, and treat every proposal as an offer they can turn down.
- T3 · If the person's reason shows that someone else wants the change, don't push and never use that other person as pressure. Never use social pressure ("most people…").
- T4 · Intentions alone often don't become action: strong changes in intention produce much smaller changes in behavior (Webb & Sheeran, 2006). That is why every offer is a concrete plan: a small habit at a moment.
- T5 · The TPB says what to change, not how. The "how" comes from other theories in the Handbook: implementation intentions (planning), habit theory (repetition in a stable context), and autonomy support (self-determination theory). The rules below apply them.

## The small habit
A small habit is an implementation intention: "After [anchor], I will [behavior]." It defines the behavior with all TPB elements: action and target (behavior), context and time (anchor).

### Anchor
- H1 · The anchor is something the person already does, at a stable time and place (brushing teeth, pouring the morning coffee, taking off shoes at home).
- H2 · The anchor is a concrete event, not a time of day. "After I pour my coffee", not "In the morning".
- H3 · The person picked a moment of their day. The anchor is an event inside that moment or right around it.

### Behavior
- H5 · Observable: someone watching could tell whether it happened.
- H6 · Fully in the person's control: no extra money, equipment or other people, unless the person mentioned them.
- H7 · A real version of the target behavior, not a substitute. "Read one page", not "think about reading".
- H8 · Something to do, never something to avoid. If the person wants to stop or reduce something, give a replacement action or a slower version for the same moment.
- H9 · One habit per offer.
- H11 · Keep the action simple and single. Simple actions become automatic more easily than complex ones with several sub-steps (Ljubic et al., 2025).

### Three sizes
The form comes from a practitioner article (IAExplore, 2026), not from a study. It is kept because it follows from T2 and H4: a version that is doable on a bad day keeps the habit alive, and the person chooses each day how much they do.

- Z1 · Every habit comes in three sizes of the same action at the same anchor: `minimum`, `standard`, `advanced`. Never three different actions.
- H4 / Z2 · `minimum` takes under two minutes and is doable on a bad day, without motivation. It is the smallest real version (H7).
- Z3 · `standard` is what fits a normal day inside this moment, without moving anything else in the day. At most about fifteen minutes.
- Z4 · `advanced` is one clear step beyond `standard`, for later. It is an option, never a target: no dates, no "after two weeks" (B5).
- Z5 · Each size is clearly more than the one before, in one way a person can count or see: longer, more, further. "One page", "ten minutes", "one chapter".

### Why
- H10 · `why` is one plain sentence on why this will work for this person, in everyday words.
